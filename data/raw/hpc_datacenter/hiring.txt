										<content:encoded><![CDATA[<h3 role="heading" aria-level="2" data-uw-rm-heading="level"><strong>DAC: Chips to Systems Conference </strong></h3>
<p>The energy and buzz at this year&#8217;s <a href="https://dac.com/2026">Design Automation Conference</a> surprised everyone — despite initial concerns about its location in Long Beach, California, which is well off the beaten path for semiconductors. Hot topics included AI automation, multiphysics simulation, compute-in-memory, chiplets, physical  AI, and the development and orchestration of AI agents from design through manufacturing.</p>
<p>At the conference:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Synopsys</strong> unveiled new autonomous <a href="https://news.synopsys.com/2026-07-27-Synopsys-Advances-Agentic-AI-Chip-Design-with-AMD-and-Microsoft">agentic AI workflows</a> for chip design, developed in collaboration with <strong>Microsoft</strong>. Synopsys also worked with <strong>Nvidia</strong> on new long-running, fully agentic workflows spanning from <a href="https://news.synopsys.com/2026-07-26-Synopsys-Showcases-Comprehensive-Autonomous-Engineering-Workflows-from-Silicon-to-Systems,-Developed-with-NVIDIA-Technology">EDA to CAE</a>.</li>
<li><strong>Cadence, </strong>in collaboration with Nvidia<strong>,</strong> introduced an agentic AI platform focused on <a href="https://community.cadence.com/cadence_blogs_8/b/corporate-news/posts/the-autonomous-chip-to-system-engineer-has-arrived">PCB and advanced packaging</a> design that coordinates specialized agents across planning, implementation, and multiphysics analysis.</li>
<li><strong>Siemens EDA</strong> introduced self-verifying <a href="https://news.siemens.com/en-us/siemens-nvidia-dac-2026/">agentic AI workflows</a> for IC design that use <strong>Nvidia</strong> AI to help long-running, domain-specific agents reason, act, and continuously validate decisions against deterministic, physics-based EDA engines.</li>
<li>Numerous companies had tools certified for <strong>Intel Foundry&#8217;s</strong> 14A and 18A-P processes, including <strong>Cadence&#8217;s</strong> AI-driven <a href="https://community.cadence.com/cadence_blogs_8/b/corporate-news/posts/cadence-certifies-ai-driven-reference-flows-for-intel-18a-p-and-intel-14a">reference flows</a>, <strong>Keysight&#8217;s</strong> <a href="https://www.keysight.com/us/en/about/newsroom/news-releases/2026/0727-pr26-090-keysight-certifies-electrmagnetic-design-software-for-intel-foundrys-latest-processes.html">electromagnetic design</a>, <strong>Siemens&#8217;</strong> <a href="https://news.siemens.com/en-us/siemens-intel-dac-2026/">IC design tools,</a> and <strong>Synopsys&#8217;</strong> AI-powered <a href="https://news.synopsys.com/2026-07-27-Synopsys-and-Intel-Foundry-Fast-Track-Customer-Readiness-from-Silicon-to-Systems-on-Intel-14A">EDA flows</a> and more.</li>
</ul>

<h3><strong>Advanced packaging</strong></h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>ASE</strong> is raising its 2026 <a href="https://www.reuters.com/world/asia-pacific/taiwans-ase-tech-raises-2026-capex-by-2-billion-strong-demand-2026-07-30/">capital expenditures</a> by $2B to about $10B due to strong demand.</li>
<li><strong>Intel Foundry</strong> is <a href="https://newsroom.intel.com/intel-foundry/intels-us-advanced-packaging-enables-next-generation-ai-semiconductors">expanding advanced packaging</a> at its Rio Rancho, New Mexico, operations using Foveros, EMIB and EMIB-T to support larger chiplet-based AI systems, with package scaling reaching roughly 8× the reticle limit today and more than 12× by 2028.</li>
<li><strong>Lens Technology</strong> and <strong>Intel</strong> announced a strategic collaboration to develop <a href="https://newsroom.intel.com/new-technologies/intel-and-lens-technology-collaborate-to-enable-advanced-semiconductor-packaging-for-the-ai-era">glass substrate-based advanced packaging</a> aimed at increasing interconnect density, performance and power efficiency for AI and data center systems.</li>
<li>As <a href="https://semiengineering.com/flat-enough-warpage-management-gets-harder-in-advanced-packaging/">packages get larger and thinner</a>, warpage is becoming a process-control problem rather than a simple flatness spec.</li>
<li>
<div class="" data-turn-id-container="a71a7273-3ec2-491b-84d8-ad756f34dc55" data-is-intersecting="true"><strong>Qnity</strong> joined <strong>imec’s</strong> semiconductor research program to accelerate development of materials for <a href="https://www.qnityelectronics.com/news/qnity-strengthens-long-term-innovation-and-advanced-packaging-roadmap.html">advanced packaging, chiplets, HBM, 3D stacking and heterogeneous integration</a>.</div>

<h3><strong>Asia developments</strong></h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>UMC</strong> approved a <a href="https://www.umc.com/en/News/press_release/Content/corporate/20260729-1">phased capacity expansion</a> that will add cleanroom space and equipment at its Phase 4 facility in Singapore while beginning construction of a new fab shell at its Tainan campus in Taiwan. The Singapore expansion will increase <strong>silicon photonics capacity</strong>, while the Tainan project will house future Phase 7 and Phase 8 facilities and expand UMC’s advanced packaging footprint for AI and edge-computing applications.</li>
<li>A 7.1-magnitude earthquake in Japan’s Kumamoto region disrupted manufacturing across Kyushu, prompting a temporary halt to operations at <a href="https://www.caranddriver.com/news/a73297750/toyota-nissan-suspend-production-japan-earthquake/">automotive</a> and chip manufacturing plants, including <a href="https://www.tel.com/news/disaster/2026/20260729_001.html">TEL </a>and <strong>TSMC</strong>&#8216;s <a href="https://asia.nikkei.com/economy/natural-disasters/kumamoto-quake-disrupts-tsmc-toyota-operations-in-southern-japan">JASM</a>.</li>
<li>China has begun producing domestically developed <a href="https://www.reuters.com/world/china/china-starts-production-home-grown-immersion-duv-chipmaking-tools-source-2026-07-28/">immersion DUV</a> lithography systems, with state-backed <strong>Shanghai Aishengna </strong>leading an effort<strong>, </strong>reports Reuters. The systems are expected to be delivered this year, although they remain well behind <strong>ASML</strong> in the throughput, overlay, yield and reliability needed for high-volume manufacturing.</li>
<li><strong>MediaTek</strong> just approved $5B in financing to expand into &#8220;from AI ASIC chips to <a href="https://www.mediatek.com/hubfs/MediaTek%20Assets/Pdfs/Quarterly%20Earnings%20Release/2026/Quarterly%20Earnings%20Release-2026Q2/Prepared%20remarks.pdf">full-scale systems and platforms</a>.&#8221;</li>
</ul>
<h3><strong>Market analysis</strong></h3>
<ul>
<li>The <strong>SIA </strong>released its <a href="https://www.semiconductors.org/2026-state-of-the-industry-report-historic-growth-amid-intensifying-global-competition/">State of the Industry Report</a>, highlighting that global chip sales reached a record $796B in 2025 and are projected to hit $1.5T in 2026. U.S.-headquartered companies held 53% of the market, while domestic semiconductor investments announced since 2020 exceeded $770B across 160 projects.</li>
<li><a href="https://www.semi.org/en/semi-press-release/semi-reports-worldwide-silicon-wafer-shipments-increase-7.4-percent-year-on-year-in-q2-2026">Global silicon wafer shipments</a> rose 7% year over year and 9% sequentially in Q2 2026, <strong>SEMI</strong> reported, driven by AI demand and improving industrial and automotive markets.</li>
<li><a href="https://www.yolegroup.com/press-release/wafer-fab-equipment-a-market-on-track-to-explode/">Wafer fab equipment sales</a> are expected to rise 25% to about $162B in 2026 and approach $220B by 2031, according to <strong>Yole Group</strong>, fueled by AI, advanced logic, memory and packaging investment.</li>
<li><strong>TrendForce</strong> expects <a href="https://www.trendforce.com/presscenter/news/20260730-13158.html">DRAM shortages</a> and rising prices to continue in 2027 as HBM and AI servers absorb capacity, while NAND supply may loosen as new fabs and higher-layer products increase output.</li>
</ul>

					<description><![CDATA[<p>For compute-intensive applications, clusters of nodes that act as a single computer offer a way to scale performance and provide a workaround for organizations that lack access to leading-edge AI chips.</p>
<p>The post <a href="https://semiengineering.com/compute-clusters-break-out-of-national-labs-to-scale-ai/">Compute Clusters Break Out Of National Labs To Scale AI</a> appeared first on <a href="https://semiengineering.com">Semiconductor Engineering</a>.</p>

										<content:encoded><![CDATA[<p><strong>Key Takeaways:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>A cluster comprises a group of interconnected servers (aka nodes) across multiple racks, connected via Ethernet and increasingly via optical links, and is ideal for workloads that are so large and require so much compute that a single rack of servers cannot do the job.</li>
<li>Design challenges include network congestion, latency and stragglers, power delivery, thermal limits, software scheduling, performance and scalability, and power and energy.</li>
<li>Clusters are already deployed in national labs for use cases such as global weather simulations, but widespread AI is pushing the solution into manufacturing, drug discovery, financial risk modeling, EDA, autonomous vehicles, and more.</li>
</ul>
<hr />
<p><strong>Experts At The Table</strong>: Semiconductor Engineering sat down to discuss compute cluster definitions, design challenges, and use cases with Satadal Bhattacharjee, global head of cloud and AI infrastructure silicon at <a href="https://semiengineering.com/entities/arm/">Arm</a>; Ashish Darbari, CEO of <a href="https://semiengineering.com/entities/axiomise/">Axiomise</a>; Moshiko Emmer, distinguished engineer at <a href="https://semiengineering.com/entities/cadence-design-systems/">Cadence</a>; Sharad Chole, chief scientist at <a href="https://semiengineering.com/entities/expedera/">Expedera</a>; Cameron Brunner, head of HPC development at <a href="https://semiengineering.com/entities/mentor-a-siemens-business/">Siemens EDA</a>; and Sumit Vishwakarma, director of strategic marketing at <a href="https://semiengineering.com/entities/synopsys-inc/">Synopsys</a>. What follows are excerpts of that discussion.</p>
<p><img data-recalc-dims="1" decoding="async" class="alignnone size-full wp-image-24279264" src="https://i0.wp.com/semiengineering.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/Screenshot-2026-07-23-at-2.38.14-PM.png?resize=2462%2C566&#038;ssl=1" alt="" width="2462" height="566" srcset="https://i0.wp.com/semiengineering.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/Screenshot-2026-07-23-at-2.38.14-PM.png?w=2462&amp;ssl=1 2462w, https://i0.wp.com/semiengineering.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/Screenshot-2026-07-23-at-2.38.14-PM.png?resize=300%2C69&amp;ssl=1 300w, https://i0.wp.com/semiengineering.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/Screenshot-2026-07-23-at-2.38.14-PM.png?resize=1024%2C235&amp;ssl=1 1024w, https://i0.wp.com/semiengineering.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/Screenshot-2026-07-23-at-2.38.14-PM.png?resize=768%2C177&amp;ssl=1 768w, https://i0.wp.com/semiengineering.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/Screenshot-2026-07-23-at-2.38.14-PM.png?resize=1536%2C353&amp;ssl=1 1536w, https://i0.wp.com/semiengineering.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/Screenshot-2026-07-23-at-2.38.14-PM.png?resize=2048%2C471&amp;ssl=1 2048w, https://i0.wp.com/semiengineering.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/Screenshot-2026-07-23-at-2.38.14-PM.png?resize=600%2C138&amp;ssl=1 600w" sizes="(max-width: 1000px) 100vw, 1000px" /></p>
<p><strong>L-R: Arm’s Bhattacharjee; Axiomise’s Darbari; Cadence’s Emmer; Expedera’s Chole; Siemens EDA’s Brunner; Synopsys’ Vishwakarma.</strong></p>
<p><strong>SE: How do you define a compute cluster, and what does a chip architect need to know? </strong></p>
<p><strong>Bhattacharjee: </strong>When we think about a compute cluster, it&#8217;s usually a group of interconnected servers across multiple racks, typically connected through Ethernet right now, though we are slowly moving to optical as well. The idea is that the workload that it&#8217;s serving is so large and it needs so much compute horsepower that single servers, or a rack of servers, cannot do the job. That&#8217;s when the need arises to stitch multiple servers together. This is also referred to as a pod, where you have multiple of these clusters together. That&#8217;s what a compute cluster typically is, and the idea is to have low latency so that network latency does not spike as the workload spans from one rack to the other. That&#8217;s very important. When traffic flows from one server to another, and from one rack to another, many optimizations are required to maintain performance efficiency without sacrificing it as this traffic flows across the cluster.</p>
<p>From a chip architect&#8217;s standpoint, they have to think about cluster behavior, not just a single server, so that the efficiency that comes with faster components doesn&#8217;t get lost due to networking bottlenecks, for example, in rack-to-rack communication. The architect needs to think about network congestion, power delivery, thermal limits, software scheduling, and stuff like that.</p>
<p><strong>Brunner: </strong>From my background of managing software, such as multiple workload managers that manage high-performance compute clusters and executing EDA workloads at massive scale, the compute cluster is a bunch of machines that get put together and linked together in a way to solve a need at a given site operator. That could be a chip designer who&#8217;s looking to really be able to design and iterate fast with a large number of developers. A lot of work needs a large amount of capability. There are also other industries, such as life sciences, manufacturing, and CAE (computer-aided engineering), where they run very large parallel simulations. Having an infrastructure that can facilitate that is important.</p>
<p>To Satadal’s point around high-speed interconnects, that&#8217;s very, very important in large-scale MPI (message passing interface) jobs. A cluster of machines can be much larger than you could ever have in a given machine, able to run very large workloads, or a very large number of small workloads to meet the needs of the end users. That&#8217;s something we see a lot in EDA. Often there will be hundreds of thousands, or millions of tasks that are used to build the flow when designing a chip. These may only run for a few seconds, but how do you run hundreds of thousands? Then you have multiple engineers running millions of jobs a day. It becomes more capacity than a single machine can handle, more data than you could ever put on a single disk. It’s about managing the networking, the storage, the overall compute capacity, and providing this parallel massive resource to drive your business forward. And a very important part is having the software on there to make sure you can map the needs of your business and of your activity to efficiently utilize the hardware and get the best return out of the infrastructure. The compute cluster is that whole ecosystem — the hardware, the software, all built together to solve the needs of the operator.</p>
<p><strong>Vishwakarma: </strong>The world is changing the cluster workloads, such as EDA workloads. How our brain evolved is similar to how the AI brain is evolving. In the past, humans did mediocre jobs, but as our brains evolved, we began doing more complex tasks — and we still only use 20 watts. Look at today compared to where AI was in 2018. The types of tasks it can do have evolved so much. From a single text prompt, you can create a one-minute video, or some people are even making movies using AI, just by using prompts. Think about how complex compute is happening behind the scenes. The LLMs have to create the next pixel to generate pixel-to-pixel, pixel-to-frame, and frames connect to become a movie, so can one GPU do it? No. Think about workloads like agentic flows. Everybody&#8217;s talking about agentic AI, where the task is not just one task. It&#8217;s a task where one agent has to compute something and give the output to the other agent, and another agent. Let&#8217;s say you have to create a research report. Right now, you give the task to the agent. &#8216;Hey, give me a research report, create a presentation.&#8217; It’s going to go to a website, look for the data, crunch the data, try to find out what is best, put it into Excel, create a graph, bring that graph into PowerPoint, make it visually appealing — you can see all the agentic flows. Because of all those, we need compute beyond the GPUs, beyond the server, beyond one node into multiple nodes, which are pods. So it becomes a whole set of compute nodes, which is one brain just doing one big task.</p>
<p><strong>Chole: </strong>My take on this is more application-centric. We are basically looking at it as if there is a compute platform available — all the hardware, connectivity, and software stack. From this platform, we can determine how applications are launched and control access in terms of network connectivity, whitelisting/blacklisting, and access ports. And all of that is given as a service. When I say services, it is not just a cloud provider. Even internally, when we need to access a very large group of machines or hardware, it has to be accessed as some sort of service, even for internal employees. That&#8217;s basically how applications can be easily deployed, and easily managed or orchestrated. There is a failover that needs to be addressed, and it&#8217;s enabling applications to be scaled, deployed seamlessly, and secured within the given framework. This is like a boundary on top of which you can say that there is an application-level cluster, which means that you&#8217;re serving a single neural network, a single LLM, for example. You might have disaggregated prefill and decode, and you&#8217;re deploying that on a set of GPUs, maybe a two-server node, or a five-server node, and that can become a cluster by itself. That can be defined as a cluster.</p>
<p><strong>Emmer:</strong> From a chip architect’s point of view, a compute cluster is not just a collection of servers. It’s a hierarchy of communication domains — die-to-die, package-to-package, accelerator-to-CPU, node-to-node, and rack-to-rack. If you don’t design the lower layers with the cluster in mind, the software ends up paying for that in latency, synchronization overhead, and power. For me, cluster architecture starts at the package boundary, not just at the network boundary. The package should be treated as the first network hop. The architectural question is not only ‘how fast is the compute?’ but ‘how many boundaries does the data cross before useful work happens?’ Chiplets matter because they let you optimize local bandwidth density while still scaling externally.</p>
<p><strong>Darbari:</strong> From my perspective, a compute cluster is a group of machines connected and managed so that, from the workload’s perspective, they operate as one much larger computing system. Each node has its own processor, memory, and software stack, while a high-speed network and cluster software coordinate them into a single logical resource. Correctness therefore depends not only on local microarchitecture, but also on the guarantees the broader system makes about communication, ordering, data visibility, and recovery from faults. As formal verification experts, we would view this verification as modeling and capturing it as explicit contracts rather than leaving it implicit among hardware, firmware, and system software.</p>
<p><strong>SE: Alibaba recently announced a 10,000-card compute cluster for a data center to serve the government, health care, and advanced manufacturing industries. While China-made AI chips currently trail behind Nvidia’s most advanced chips, large-scale computing clusters with innovative and efficient network architecture can improve computing performance, enabling China to catch up. Is this a solution for U.S. companies too? What are the tradeoffs?</strong></p>
<p><strong>Chole: </strong>When you&#8217;re talking about a 10,000-node cluster that Alibaba is launching, that becomes a very big challenge. Other than training jobs, there is no single job that you can run on that big of a cluster — currently at least. Maybe there are big companies with that much inference demand, but training typically requires large clusters to be available and pipelined. When you&#8217;re thinking about this problem, we think about parallelization — how the work can be broken down in terms of different parallelization strategies, and what kind of plumbing needs to be done. This is plumbing for data coming in, communication between the nodes, how the output needs to be rendered, what state needs to be saved, and what are the storage accesses? The entire connectivity between so many nodes becomes a critical point.</p>
<p><strong>Darbari:</strong> Alibaba&#8217;s 10,000-card cluster is a system-level answer to a device-level gap. If individual chips are not leading-edge, performance can still be recovered by scaling out aggressively and by building a fabric and software stack that makes many devices operate as one large AI platform. That strategy is valid, but it is not unique. I believe that U.S. hyperscalers have already built systems in this class. Microsoft has publicly described an Azure supercomputer for OpenAI with around 10,000 GPUs connected as a single coordinated system. AWS offers EC2 UltraClusters designed to scale AI training to tens of thousands of GPUs, including 10K‑class deployments. Google has likewise built very large TPU pod systems with thousands of TPUs for frontier‑scale training, even though the accelerator technology differs.</p>
<p>So the core idea is already established in the U.S. — use large, tightly connected clusters to deliver competitive effective compute. The more meaningful difference is not the headline device count but the maturity of the surrounding stack. In the Chinese case, the emphasis is often on offsetting a per-chip disadvantage through system architecture and networking. In the U.S., those large clusters tend to sit inside a more mature ecosystem of tooling, software frameworks, operations, and long-running experience with distributed infrastructure at scale. The tradeoff is therefore straightforward. Scaling out can compensate for weaker individual devices, but it raises complexity sharply in performance tuning, failure handling, software orchestration, and security assurance. If those system-level behaviors are not specified rigorously, more scale can magnify uncertainty rather than reduce it. If they are specified and validated well, scale becomes a disciplined architectural advantage rather than a fragile workaround.</p>
<p><strong>SE: Compute clusters are common in AI laboratories, life sciences, high-frequency trading, and other specialized applications. They are increasingly common among hyperscalers. Are we going to see them becoming more common in other sectors, and why?</strong></p>
<p><strong>Brunner: </strong>We&#8217;re talking a lot about AI uses, and what’s driving that right now. Cluster services are this hybrid intersection, which we’re seeing with services being deployed on traditional HPC clusters. For example, one of our big customers is Argonne National Laboratory, and their cluster that we run on Aurora has 65,000 GPUs on it. They will use that cluster for one job, such as world-scale weather simulation and these types of things. There are use cases for massive clusters, these leadership-class facilities, and their primary purpose of building these systems is for running work that can&#8217;t be run anywhere else. That&#8217;s the motivation. Where else could you do this simulation? That&#8217;s what they want to run. So typically it&#8217;s a much different usage pattern [than in other sectors]. [National Labs] do solve these very big challenges. It is something that we are doing in the U.S., and we have several of these exascale compute clusters already operational. We have three of them. It is a common use case that’s different from traditional EDA, with scientific computing as its foundation.</p>
<p><strong>Bhattacharjee: </strong>I can resonate with that, because I worked on HPC systems earlier, and what I&#8217;ve seen is that most of those HPC systems used to be deployed by large national labs for weather simulation, and different kinds of computational physical dynamics or CPD workloads. There are all kinds of heavy math use cases, where you need tons of compute to crunch math. Now, in the age of AI, that has become mainstream. That&#8217;s the reason why we are seeing the data centers going through a complete change or shift, where those 30- and 50-kilowatt racks, which is what hyperscalers traditionally used to use, are growing to hundreds of kilowatts of racks. The systems are no longer capable of delivering the compute required with air-cooled systems, so we are talking about liquid cooling. That&#8217;s what we are seeing —  this huge shift, and this is not just confined to national labs anymore. Now we are talking about the hyperscalers building out data centers globally, and the rest of the industry catching up, and there is a push toward sovereignty because every country wants to ensure that they have their own LLMs for government and security and all of that. They don&#8217;t want to depend on other countries&#8217; clouds. So we are seeing this build-out with high CapEx spend on acquiring land, getting all those systems stitched together, and then the software to run on them. I feel that&#8217;s going to be across different use cases.</p>
<p>It will touch manufacturing, drug discovery, financial risk modeling, EDA, autonomous vehicles, you name it. Every sector is going to leverage that, because the level of productivity we are seeing — we are still in the early stages — is already overwhelming. The amount of time that was required to do things is now shrinking, so you can get more done. We are on this journey, and it&#8217;s going to be maybe a decade-long or longer, where all of these systems will be built out. And then you&#8217;ll have these models, which are getting more and more intelligent, to do the work that used to take a long time.</p>
<p><strong>Vishwakarma: </strong>I completely agree. For the frontier model itself, the training and the new model development, fundamentally, if you look at the workloads from the past 10 years, these were high-performance computing for weather predictions, and those were used for a lot of research projects. But what has changed is that for AI compute, the workload itself is heavily dependent on this one operation, which is matrix multiplication, the MAC (multiply-accumulate) operation. That&#8217;s why [HPC] pivoted to the GPUs, and not just a single GPU, but a matrix of GPUs, eight GPUs in one server. We are expanding this for clusters, but if you look at it from the bottom up, why are these data centers consuming so much power? It’s the GPU-to-GPU communication — there&#8217;s so much data transfer happening. The GPU to the memory — there&#8217;s a lot of data transfer happening, and the reason for that is because of these training models. You have weights and everything, and then you shift the weights in there to make sure that we have the best frontier model. Everybody is fighting for that. And the same thing is kind of happening on the inferencing side. What is different now is that power consumption has become the bottleneck, along with bandwidth—the latency. These are the two important challenges. Of course, there is reliability and safety, but power consumption, if you look at the amount of power these data centers are consuming. It’s the power equivalent to the city’s consumption. These needs are bringing new challenges, which did not exist before.</p>
<p><strong>Emmer</strong><strong>: </strong>I agree clusters will become more mainstream, but not as one uniform architecture. As adoption broadens, we’ll see more domain-specific cluster composition — different memory footprints, different latency tolerances, and different ratios of control-heavy versus tensor-heavy work. Architecturally, that argues for modularity. And that’s exactly where chiplet-based design becomes strategic.</p>
<p><strong>Darbari:</strong> As more industries run into the limits of a single large server in terms of performance, memory footprint, or service availability, scale-out architectures become the practical next step. What was once concentrated in hyperscale and high-performance computing is increasingly relevant to enterprises adopting AI, large-scale analytics, and always-on digital services.</p>
<p>Clusters are attractive because they let organizations expand capacity incrementally, maintain service through node-level failures, avoiding a single point of failure, and often achieve a better cost-performance balance than continually moving to larger monolithic systems. Then, formal verification is needed because as clusters spread, informal descriptions of behavior become less sustainable. Multi-team and multi-vendor systems benefit from precise definitions of consistency, failover, and isolation so that implementations can be checked against a common intent.</p>

					<description><![CDATA[<p>As packages get larger and thinner, warpage is becoming a process-control problem rather than a simple flatness spec. </p>
<p>The post <a href="https://semiengineering.com/flat-enough-warpage-management-gets-harder-in-advanced-packaging/">Flat Enough? Warpage Management Gets Harder In Advanced Packaging</a> appeared first on <a href="https://semiengineering.com">Semiconductor Engineering</a>.</p>
]]></description>
										<content:encoded><![CDATA[<p><b>Key Takeaways:</b></p>
<ul>
<li>Warpage is shifting from a global bow specification to a process-dependent surface-shape problem that affects bonding, alignment, lithography, reliability, and downstream assembly.</li>
<li>Panel-level packaging, glass carriers/substrates, hybrid bonding, and HBM integration are making warpage harder to control because stress accumulates across materials, thermal steps, and larger geometries.</li>
<li>Managing “flat enough” will require earlier shape metrology, better material data, process-aware simulation, and stack designs that balance CTE and stress before deformation becomes yield loss.</li>
</ul>
<hr />
<p>Flatness is no longer simply a matter of whether a substrate bows beyond a specified limit. Warpage now shows up as a collection of local shape signatures, potentially affecting everything from bonding and alignment to yield and reliability.</p>
<p>That shift is forcing manufacturers to connect material behavior, process history, surface metrology, and simulation earlier in the flow, because &#8220;flat enough” is a moving target that depends on what the next process step demands.</p>
<p>The first challenge is agreeing on where deviations from flatness originate. In advanced packaging, the same flatness number can hide very different failure mechanisms, depending on whether the deformation comes from substrate bow, local die tilt, redistribution layer variation, bonding, CMP, localized stress, or thermal expansion mismatch.</p>
<p>“The industry needs to stop treating flatness as one global mechanical number,” said Adam Chuang, vice president of business development for optics at Wooptix. “In advanced packaging, flatness is a composition of multiple different length scales that contribute from the process itself. Some of the signatures are very low-order bow or warp, but others contribute directly to local slope, die-to-die coplanarity, surface deformations caused by bonding, CMP, redistribution layers, thermal effects, or material mismatches.”</p>
<p>That distinction changes the question from whether a package is flat to which shape components of bonding, CMP, coefficient of thermal expansion (CTE) mismatches, and thermal effects contribute most to yield, reliability, and final system performance.</p>
<p><b>Why warpage is getting worse</b><br />
Several converging forces are making warpage harder to control. AI and HPC packages are growing larger, placing more silicon area on organic or glass-based structures. Chiplet integration and HBM stacking add heterogeneous materials with different CTEs. Hybrid bonding and fine-pitch interconnects narrow the tolerance window for surface deviation. And panel-level processing amplifies every thermal and mechanical effect across a geometry in which even small material-property differences produce large deformations.</p>
<p><img data-recalc-dims="1" loading="lazy" decoding="async" class="alignnone size-large wp-image-24279257" src="https://i0.wp.com/semiengineering.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/Image-from-iOS-8.gif?resize=1024%2C608&#038;ssl=1" alt="" width="1024" height="608" srcset="https://i0.wp.com/semiengineering.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/Image-from-iOS-8.gif?resize=1024%2C608&amp;ssl=1 1024w, https://i0.wp.com/semiengineering.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/Image-from-iOS-8.gif?resize=300%2C178&amp;ssl=1 300w, https://i0.wp.com/semiengineering.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/Image-from-iOS-8.gif?resize=768%2C456&amp;ssl=1 768w, https://i0.wp.com/semiengineering.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/Image-from-iOS-8.gif?resize=600%2C356&amp;ssl=1 600w" sizes="auto, (max-width: 1000px) 100vw, 1000px" /></p>
<p><b>Fig. 1: ASE’s automated panel-level packaging line supports</b><b> a</b><b> 310mm × 310mm format</b>.</p>
<p>At the panel scale, the entire material stack drives warpage. That makes materials selection and process sequencing inseparable. Every polymer in the stack has its own transition temperature. Every metal layer has its own CTE, thickness, and pattern density. Every cure step can introduce residual stress before the next layer is added. The result is a structure whose mechanical state at any given moment reflects the entire thermal and chemical history that preceded it.</p>
<p>The transition from wafer to panel is where those interdependencies become hardest to manage. What works on a 300mm wafer does not automatically scale to larger rectangular panels. Coating uniformity and stress distribution both become more difficult to control over a larger area, and the cumulative effect of mismatched materials compounds across a geometry that offers no natural boundary to contain it. Perhaps these are some of the reasons the industry is starting small, with 310 x 310mm panels.</p>
<p>“Going from wafer to panel creates material stability issues from non-uniform coating and managing higher stress and warpage levels,” said Hamed Gholami Derami, business development engineer for packaging solutions at <a href="https://semiengineering.com/entities/brewer-science/">Brewer Science</a>. “New temporary bonding materials with improved thermal and mechanical stability are needed for panel-level packaging.”</p>
<p>That constraint becomes especially acute for HBM applications, where DRAM dies must be thinned aggressively. The total thickness variation of the temporary bonding layer sets the floor for how uniformly that thinning can be achieved, and any non-uniformity introduced there can propagate into subsequent process steps.</p>
<p><b>Measuring shape, not just bow</b><br />
Traditional warpage metrology can detect warping in a package. But advanced packaging increasingly needs &#8220;shape metrology,&#8221; the dense surface data that can connect deformation signatures to process fingerprints and yield outcomes. Getting more data points across the surface — and early enough to take action — matters more than sharpening any single measurement. A high-resolution tool that samples sparsely can miss the shape signatures that determine whether a package will bond, align, or yield correctly.</p>
<p>“Older flatness metrics are not enough,” said Cheung. “That’s why we develop a high-density data map for the wafer itself, and in the future, the panel. It is designed to fully describe whether these vertically integrated systems can ultimately perform in a bondable way, in an alignable way, and in a reliable way, and then have high yield.”</p>
<p>The value is not simply seeing that a package has warped. It is in collecting enough surface data to translate raw measurements into process intelligence and identifying which signatures are process-induced, where in the flow they originate, and what corrective action is needed.</p>
<p>The catch is that shape metrology becomes useful only when it is correlated with device characterization and converted into a controllable action plan. Most metrology today is target-based. It checks a specific feature against a reference at a specific moment. What advanced packaging needs is repeated, high-density surface-shape measurement at key process handoffs, so that deformation introduced in one step can be accounted for before the next step begins. As packages grow larger and tolerances tighten, the cost of carrying a poor assumption forward compounds with every subsequent operation.</p>
<p><b>Glass improves, but doesn’t eliminate the problem</b><br />
Glass carriers and substrates are attractive for advanced packaging because of their stiffness, surface smoothness, optical transparency for laser-based debonding, and their tunable CTE across a broad range. That last property is particularly significant. Unlike organic build-up substrates, which offer limited CTE flexibility, glass can be formulated to match a wide range of integration requirements.</p>
<p>“Glass has a tunable CTE, ranging from 3.4 to 10.6 ppm per degree,” said Wiwy Wudjud, program manager at <a href="https://semiengineering.com/entities/ase/">ASE</a>. “That’s very flexible, depending on what kind of materials or process requirements you have.”</p>
<p>That tunability, combined with glass’s smoothness and optical transparency for UV and laser debonding, is why glass carriers are becoming increasingly important in advanced packaging flows that require stable support, fine alignment, and clean debonding. But even at the 300mm wafer scale, the processing challenges for glass are significant. At the panel scale, those challenges multiply.</p>
<p>Glass also does not resolve the underlying CTE imbalance challenge in heterogeneous stacks. The more silicon area a package carries (e.g., chiplets, HBM cubes, bridge die, etc.), the more lopsided the thermal expansion equation becomes, regardless of what the substrate is made of. The answer is not forcing a warped structure flat, but designing the stack so that the competing CTEs are better distributed from the start.</p>
<p>“If you just pile all your low CTEs in one place and all your high CTEs in another, you’re just asking for problems,” said Mike Kelly, vice president of chiplets and FCBGA integration at <a href="https://semiengineering.com/entities/amkor-technology/">Amkor</a>. “Physics isn’t going to change. So we’re going to be seeing new concepts that balance the CTEs across the cross-section of the package.”</p>
<p>Glass’s tunability helps, and CTE-matched glass substrates are coming. But glass only addresses part of the problem. Unless the whole stack architecture is designed with CTE balance in mind, warpage, die shift, cracking, and stress concentration can reappear elsewhere in the flow. A glass substrate with a well-matched CTE sitting under a multi-chiplet module with eight HBM cubes still has to cope with significant asymmetry, and managing that through mechanical constraint alone using stiffener rings or retainer structures just adds stress elsewhere. It doesn&#8217;t remove stress from the overall system.</p>
<p><b>Hybrid bonding makes surface control less forgiving</b><br />
Hybrid bonding raises the stakes for surface control because the tolerance window is much narrower than in solder-based interconnects. There are no organics at the interface to provide compliance. Particles, surface topology, and CMP process variation can all become yield limiters in ways that they are not in conventional front-end processes.</p>
<p>The surface also has to be engineered for the anneal step that follows bonding. Copper and oxide behave differently under heat, and the CMP process has to account for both.</p>
<p>“You have to manage that surface topology,” said Amkor’s Kelly. “Oxides want to be bone flat because the chemical mechanism that pulls the oxides together is really what bonds. It’s not the copper. The copper is just along for the ride. It’s getting pressed together in the anneal step.”</p>
<p>The copper starts slightly recessed relative to the oxide. As the temperature rises during annealing, copper expands due to its high CTE relative to silicon. Applying too much compressive force during that step plastically deforms the copper. The result is that “flat enough” is process-specific. A surface that passes inspection at one step may not satisfy the requirements of the oxide bonding chemistry or the copper anneal that follows. Warpage and surface topology here are not just mechanical measurements, they are direct determinants of whether the bond forms correctly.</p>
<p><b>Modeling must follow the process</b><br />
Process simulation for warpage is only useful if it tracks stress accumulation through each step of the build rather than just predicting the final package state. Every thermal excursion, whether in processing, test, or field operation, loads the same CTE mismatch that drove warpage during assembly. Glass, copper, and silicon respond to that loading very differently.</p>
<p>“When the temperature goes up and down, either in testing, manufacturing, or deployment, we know the effect of CTE,” said Lang Lin, principal product manager at <a href="https://semiengineering.com/entities/synopsys-inc/">Synopsys</a>. “We are talking about glass, copper, and silicon, which have very different CTEs. Those are totally different materials, and thermal cycling can create major cracking and reliability concerns, especially around how much stress the glass can tolerate during those thermal cycle events.”</p>
<p>That is where process simulation becomes more difficult. The model has to take material properties as inputs and track how stress accumulates across each step of the build. But there is a persistent gap between what the simulation needs and what manufacturers are willing to provide. A substrate supplier’s process recipe and exact material formulation are competitive assets, and sharing them with a third-party simulation tool is not straightforward.</p>
<p>&#8220;Our simulation tool needs the substrate manufacturers&#8217; material properties information,&#8221; said Lin. &#8220;These may include the measurement-calibrated material properties for building encrypted technology files.&#8221;</p>
<p>Without that correlation, most models rely on generic material libraries that are still useful for understanding relative stress behaviors and testing design directions, but which are less precise for predicting actual process outcomes. The practical advice is to run the simulation with the best available material data before committing to a physical experiment.</p>
<p><b>Correction strategies: Flatten, compensate, or redesign</b><br />
Once warpage is measured and understood, manufacturers face a choice — try to eliminate it, work around it, or design the process so it arrives at the right shape at the right moment. Each approach has a different cost and a different set of tradeoffs, and most production flows use some combination of all three.</p>
<p>The most direct mechanical approach is physical constraint, such as vacuum chucks that pull a warped substrate flat during lithography or bonding, or stiffener rings and retainer structures that resist deformation during thermal excursions. These are widely used and often effective, but they have limits. A vacuum chuck cannot hold a substrate that has warped beyond a certain threshold without creating contact problems that affect the process, and stiffeners that constrain a package from deforming do not eliminate the stress. They redistribute it, often concentrating it at solder joints or other reliability-critical interfaces.</p>
<p>A second approach is compensation — measuring the surface shape and adjusting subsequent process steps accordingly. Lithography tools can apply corrections to the exposure field based on measured bow. Die placement equipment can offset pick-and-place coordinates based on a surface map. The assumption is not that the package will be flat, but that its shape is known well enough to make corrections in the next step. This is where high-density surface metrology becomes directly actionable. It’s no longer just a quality check. It&#8217;s an input to the process control loop.</p>
<p>The third approach, and the one the industry is increasingly moving toward, is redesigning the stack and the thermal process so that warpage is minimized structurally rather than corrected after the fact.</p>
<p>Thinning the silicon is one example. A thinner die is mechanically weaker as a low-CTE stiffener, meaning the rest of the material stack has greater influence on the final shape and can be engineered to produce a flatter result. The tradeoff is thermal. Thinner silicon manages lateral heat spreading less effectively, which is a real constraint for high-power data center devices.</p>
<p>“You make the impact of the low-CTE silicon less if there’s thinner silicon to permit some warpage,” said Kelly. “In the extreme, you can make it super thin, but then it’s just going to follow the shape of whatever it’s attached to.”</p>
<p>The most sophisticated approach is engineering the thermal profile of the fabrication process itself to minimize stress at operating temperature rather than at room temperature. Most packages are assembled and measured at room temperature, but they run at 90 to 100 degrees Celsius, or higher. During fabrication, every cure step, lamination, and thermal cycle leaves residual stress in the stack. If the fabrication process is designed deliberately, the thermal expansion at operating temperature could counteract those residual stresses rather than add to them, leaving the package in a more balanced mechanical state when it is actually operating in the field.</p>
<p>The catch is that a package optimized this way could measure worse at room temperature than one built to conventional thermal profiles. It may fall outside standard bow or flatness specifications on the inspection bench even though it performs correctly in the field. That is a direct challenge to how acceptance criteria are written today, which were developed around room-temperature measurement and the assumption that a flatter package at assembly is always better.</p>
<p>As operating temperatures rise with AI workloads, the industry may need to reconsider whether room-temperature flatness is the right metric. The goal is not a package that is flat on the assembly line. It is a package that is flat, or at least within tolerance, where it matters, at the temperature and load conditions of field operation.</p>
<p>“You’re going to see more people working on stress/strain profiles that are optimized for operating temperature rather than room temperature,” said Kelly. “A package might have more initial stress than you want when it comes off the line, but by the time it ramps up to 90 or 100 degrees in operation, it’s actually in a lower stress state. That’s the goal — managing the thermal history during fabrication so the package arrives at its best mechanical condition when it’s actually running. Of course, this presumes that you can pass JEDEC package-level reliability testing, which also covers the temperature extremes.”</p>
<p>These correction strategies depend on earlier measurements with more information. A simple bow number at the end of the line tells you whether you passed or failed. A dense surface map taken at each process handoff tells you which step created the problem, how it evolved, and what the next step needs to account for.</p>
<p>“It’s not just flatness that helps customers solve their manufacturability issues,” said Wooptix’ Cheung. “Certain shape methodologies can help them debug process issues. Manufacturers are suffering from these exotic structures and have to deal with them through metrology, and shape would be the most direct way of addressing such a challenge.”</p>
<p><b>What “flat enough” actually means</b><br />
The answer to the question of what “flat enough” looks like is not a universal number. It depends on the next step in the process flow. A package that meets a simple bow specification may still cause problems in hybrid bonding, where local oxide surface conditions, copper recess, particles, and nanoscale topography all affect whether the bond forms correctly. A panel that appears acceptable after RDL deposition may have accumulated sufficient residual stress to cause die shift during the next thermal excursion. A substrate that is flat at room temperature may be anything but flat at operating temperature, which is what really matters.</p>
<p>The more useful framing, as the industry is learning, is whether the current shape of a package is compatible with everything that has to happen next, and whether that shape can be measured early enough, with enough spatial density and process context, to allow corrections before the next step locks in the deformation permanently. That is the shift the industry is navigating, from flatness as a pass/fail inspection checkpoint to shape as a continuous variable in a manufacturing control system.</p>
<p>As packages get larger, thinner, and more heterogeneous, the cost of getting that question wrong rises sharply. A panel loaded with chiplets and HBM, costing tens of thousands of dollars per unit, leaves little margin for a warpage-driven yield problem discovered late in the process. The pressure to understand and manage shape earlier, through the integration of materials, process design, metrology, and simulation, is only going to increase.</p>

<p><strong>Abstract Excerpt:</strong> “This paper presents SPARC, an automated framework for pre-silicon PSCL evaluation and root-cause analysis. SPARC leverages macro-cell-level Information Flow Tracking (IFT) augmented with enhanced shadow logic that tags switching activity originating from secret-dependent data. By isolating this activity, SPARC applies statistical leakage tests to detect PSCL, while simultaneously attributing the leakage to specific hardware signals and mapping those signals to the corresponding software instructions. ”</p>
